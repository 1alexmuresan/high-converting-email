# Follow-ups

About 55% of replies come from follow-ups, not the initial email. About 48% of
salespeople never follow up once.

---

## Default cadence

**Three touches: day 0, day 3, day 7.**

| Touch | Day | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial | 0 | Full four-step email. Maximum personalization investment. |
| Follow-up 1 | +3 | Short human ping plus a reframe. New angle, same problem. |
| Follow-up 2 | +7 | Value or proof touch. Doubles as the soft close. |

Waiting three days before the first follow-up increases response by up to 31% versus
following up sooner. Best days are Tuesday through Thursday; Thursday peaks around
6.87% reply rate. Best times are 9–11am or 1–3pm in their local time. Avoid Monday
mornings and Friday afternoons.

---

## Why start with three, not five

Longer sequences do produce more replies in aggregate: 4–7 email campaigns hit around
27% reply rate versus 9% for 1–3 email campaigns. But that math assumes the copy is
good.

Sending five emails on copy you haven't validated maximizes spam complaints and burns
sending infrastructure. By the fourth follow-up, response rates drop about 55% and spam
complaints triple.

So: start at two or three touches. Track reply rate. Once the campaign is
over-performing — say it's at 4.8% on three touches — add a fourth. It'll lift to
roughly 6%. Add a fifth and you'll get to roughly 6.8%. Each addition lifts performance
while the underlying copy is already proven, which keeps block risk and mailbox health
manageable.

Add sequence length as a reward for good copy, not as a substitute for it.

---

## Angle rotation

Each follow-up must stand alone, because they may not have read the previous one. Each
adds exactly **one** new thing. That constraint forces genuine angle changes.

| Touch | Angle | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial | Personalized hook + core value prop + soft CTA | Introduce problem and solution |
| Follow-up 1 | Different angle or a plain reframe. New stat, new insight, or just a human ping. | Second shot at relevance |
| Follow-up 2 | Social proof from a company like theirs, or a free asset | Build credibility, or give value |
| Follow-up 3 (if added) | New insight, industry trend, useful resource | Demonstrate expertise |
| Follow-up 4 (if added) | Breakup. Acknowledge silence, leave the door open. | Trigger loss aversion |

## Follow-up 1: keep it small

The most common mistake is over-engineering the first follow-up. People think they need
a case study, deep personalization, and a reference to the prospect's third cousin.
They don't.

Real humans send short pings:

> Hey Pete, just checking in on this. Let me know if you have questions.

> Hey Pete, did this get lost in your inbox?

> Hey Pete, are you cool man? Get back to me, let me know what you think.

Slightly more structured, still short:

> Hey Pete, checking in on the [thing]. TLDR if this is the first time you're seeing
> it: [one-line reframe of the offer]. Happy to just send it over if it's easier.
>
> Alex

Two or three sentences. New subject line. That's it.

What real humans do *not* send:

> Peter, while analyzing Left Click I noticed you were spending significantly on
> platforms you didn't have to be. Our recent case study shows AAA Corp saved over
> $10,000 by implementing our solution.

Nobody talks like this. Newsletter-style follow-ups break the one-to-one frame the
initial email spent all its effort establishing.

---

## Follow-up 2: value or proof

This is where the case study goes, or the free asset, or the specific insight. It's the
last scheduled touch in a three-email sequence, so it also has to close cleanly without
sounding like an ultimatum.

Proof version:

> Hey Pete, one more and I'll leave you alone.
>
> We just wrapped with [company like theirs]. [Specific result] in [timeframe]. Same
> setup as yours from what I can tell.
>
> If it's useful I'll send you the breakdown. If not, no worries at all, and good luck
> with [their thing].
>
> Alex

Give version:

> Hey Pete, last one from me.
>
> Regardless of whether we work together, here's the thing I'd fix first: [specific,
> genuinely useful insight]. Takes about 20 minutes and it's usually worth a few
> thousand a month.
>
> If you want me to just do it, say the word. Otherwise, good luck out there.
>
> Alex

## The breakup email

If you run a longer sequence, the final touch is the breakup. It works on loss
aversion: removing the pressure while creating scarcity by withdrawing. Close.com
reports 10–15% response rates on breakups with cold prospects.

Structure:
1. Acknowledge you've reached out a few times
2. Validate that they may not be interested
3. State this is the last one for now
4. Leave the door open

> I haven't heard back, so I'll assume now isn't the right time. Before I close the
> loop: [one-sentence insight or resource]. If that changes things, just reply.
> Otherwise no hard feelings, and good luck with [their goal].

The 1-2-3 format, which reduces reply friction to near zero:

> Since I haven't heard back, I'll keep it simple. Reply with a number:
>
> 1. Interested, let's talk
> 2. Not now, check back in 3 months
> 3. Not interested, please stop

**If you send a breakup, honor it.** Do not contact them again. The technique works
because it's credible.

## Phrases that kill reply rates

- "I never heard back" — 12% drop in meeting booking rate
- "Just checking in" — zero value, signals laziness
- "Bumping this to the top of your inbox" — presumptuous
- "Did you see my last email?" — guilt-tripping
- "Following up on my previous message" — generic, adds nothing
- "As per my last email" — hostile
- "I'll take your silence as a no" — passive-aggressive ultimatum

Note that "just checking in" appears in the human-ping examples above. The difference is
whether something follows it. "Hey Pete, just checking in on this, let me know if
you've got questions" is a human message. "Just checking in!" as the entire email is
the failure mode.

---

## CTA friction by seniority

The higher up the org chart, the less friction you can ask for.

**Executives and founders:** curiosity-driven, near-zero effort. "Worth a look?" /
"Curious?" / "Want me to send it?"

**Mid-level managers:** more specific value is fine. "Want me to walk through how
[Company] saved 15 hours a week?"

**Technical roles:** precise, no fluff. Respect their intelligence. State the mechanism.

---

## Subject lines across the sequence

Use a different subject on each touch. Two reasons: it avoids the thread looking like a
nagging chain, and it gives you free subject-line testing across the campaign.

Some senders prefer to reply in-thread on the first follow-up, which preserves context.
Either works. Test it as a variable rather than assuming.
