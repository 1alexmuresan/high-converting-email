# Psychology

None of this is invented. It's documented behavioral science, mostly organized by
Cialdini and the behavioral economics literature. The point isn't to memorize the list.
It's to recognize the patterns well enough to use them without thinking.

---

## The seven principles of saying yes to a stranger

Score every draft against these. One or two out of seven is a weak email regardless of
how it reads.

### 1. Give first

Providing something valuable creates obligation, lowers resistance, and disarms
skepticism before you ask for anything. This is why restaurants bring mints with the
bill and Costco hands out samples.

In outbound: offer an insight, an audit, a free asset, a fix. The ask is inferred
without being made, so the reader assigns you positive value without the negatives of a
sales approach.

> Noticed your tracking pixel is misconfigured. It's costing you somewhere in the
> 10-20k a month range on attribution. Here's how to fix it.

An email that only takes — "hop on a 15-minute call" — is a withdrawal from their day
dressed as an offer. A call is not a gift.

### 2. Micro-commitments

Escalation of commitment. A small yes makes the next slightly larger yes feel natural.
The more times someone says yes before the real ask, the higher the odds they say yes
to it.

You cannot open with "want to pay me $4,000?" You can open with "watch this 60-second
video, and if it's not useful, tell me to screw off and I'll never message again."

Ladder: watch → reply → call → video call → proposal → close.

### 3. Social proof

Humans are herd-consensus animals. Under maximum uncertainty — which is exactly what a
message from a stranger is — reliance on social proof goes up, not down.

Three requirements:
- **Specific numbers.** "We generated $112,482 last week" beats "we make a lot of money
  with this."
- **Named or near-named parties.** "An IT business in Tennessee, about a 15-minute
  drive from you" beats "a client."
- **Matched reference group.** The more overlap between your case study and the
  prospect — industry, size, geography, business model — the more the proof lands. A
  smaller result from their world beats a bigger result from someone else's.

Deliver it as a throwaway line, not a testimonial block. The best social proof in cold
email is barely emphasized.

### 4. Authority

Credentials, renowned accomplishments, borrowed credibility, and confidence in the
writing itself. Doctors sign with MD for a reason.

Match the authority to the audience. Behavioral neuroscience credentials mean nothing to
a plumbing contractor; "I'm a Google Partner" means everything. The wrong credential is
worse than none, because it signals you don't know who you're talking to.

Signal confidence in the prose. "Well, I believe I could maybe help you" undoes
whatever authority you just established. "I can absolutely help with this, because I
just did it for X" combines authority and social proof in one line.

### 5. Rapport

Shared context of any kind: industry, city, background, hobby, a dog breed. Be specific.

Half of rapport is **implicit** — mirroring communication style, message length,
punctuation, and register. Lowercase to a startup founder. Full sentences to a
manufacturer. The goal is that the reader feels like you're sitting across from them at
a bar rather than presenting to them.

### 6. Scarcity

Limited availability or a real deadline. Pair it with give-first.

The constraint must be genuine. Fake urgency trips BS detectors instantly. The
constraints that work best are the ones that admit a limitation on your side: your own
capacity, your schedule, the number of clients you can onboard this month, a proposal
price that holds for seven days.

This is the least-used of the seven principles, which makes it an edge when used well.

### 7. Shared identity

The strongest of the seven. Common ground that says "one of us": same industry, same
values, same struggles, same vocabulary.

Vocabulary is the cheapest and most effective lever. "CTR," "subs," "thumbs" to a
YouTuber. "NPEs" to a dentist. "TAM," "ACV" to a sales leader. Using the words an
insider uses does more than any explicit claim of understanding.

Also: shared hardship. If you're pitching a business owner who's been through it, and
you've been through it, say so.

## Buyer biases worth exploiting

**Loss aversion.** Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good.
"You're wasting $2,300 a month" outperforms "you could make $2,300 a month." Frame the
cost of inaction, not the upside of action.

**Hyperbolic discounting.** Immediate beats future. "In your inbox within 24 hours"
beats "ROI within 6 months."

**Zero-price effect.** Free isn't just cheap, it's psychologically different. The jump
from $1 to $0 is bigger than $2 to $1. This is why the free-asset offer converts.

**Status-quo bias.** Change feels risky. Reduce the perceived cost of switching. "You
don't have to change anything on your end."

**Regret aversion.** Guarantees, money-back, no-commitment language all remove the fear
of a bad decision rather than adding to the upside.

**Zeigarnik effect.** Open loops occupy the mind. A subject line that raises a question
it doesn't answer is this principle in action.

**Pratfall effect.** Competent people get more likable when they show a small flaw.
Admitting a limitation increases trust. "I'm not the cheapest." / "I only take three of
these a month." / "I know this is completely out of left field."

**Curse of knowledge.** Your offer is obvious to you and opaque to them. Test whether a
stranger to your industry can tell what you're offering.

**Availability heuristic.** A vivid, specific case study makes the outcome feel
achievable. Vague claims don't.

**Mimetic desire.** People want what other people want. Mentioning that others in their
space are already doing this works without needing to be a hard pitch.

**Anchoring.** The first number sets the frame. If you mention a large result before
your offer, your offer reads as modest.

**Foot-in-the-door.** A small request first, then escalate. This is micro-commitment
stated as a technique.

**Reciprocity.** Give-first, stated as a principle.

**Paradox of choice / Hick's Law.** More options means slower decisions and more
abandonment. One CTA. Never two.

**Activation energy.** The energy to *start* stops people even when the task is easy
overall. "Just send me a title" is near-zero activation energy. "Fill out this intake
form" is not.

**BJ Fogg behavior model.** Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt. All three must be
present. A compelling offer with a hard first step doesn't convert. An easy first step
with no reason doesn't either.

## Stated versus revealed preference

Prospects will tell you what they want and then behave differently. "The customer is
always right" holds for what they *do*, not what they *say*.

If someone tells you your email was too long but the long variant has the higher reply
rate, the reply rate is right. Optimize against behavior. This is why iteration beats
intuition, including your own — copy you're sure will flop routinely outperforms copy
you love.

## The ethics line

These principles get used on you every day by every company you interact with.
Understanding them isn't manipulation. Using them to sell something real to someone who
benefits from it is normal commerce.

The line is falsehood. Fabricated results, invented case studies, fake scarcity, fake
urgency, and manufactured personalization are not persuasion techniques. They're lies,
they get detected, and they destroy the trust the entire method depends on. Every
principle above works better when what you're saying is true.
