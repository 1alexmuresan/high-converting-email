# Subject lines

The subject line has exactly one job: buy the click. It does not sell. If you catch
yourself selling in the subject line, you've made a mistake.

---

## Plausible deniability

The governing principle. Give enough to spark curiosity, not enough to answer the
question without opening.

If the subject line answers "who is this and why should I care," the reader has no
reason to click. So you withhold that deliberately. The reader should be able to
plausibly believe you are:

- a long-time fan
- someone from their past
- a potential partner or vendor
- someone who wants to buy their business
- a podcast booker
- someone with a friendly comment
- someone whose life their work changed

Anything except a salesperson. The moment the subject reads as a pitch, it gets
mentally filed before it gets opened.

---

## The rules

**2–4 words.** Two-word subjects get about 60% more opens than five-word. Going from
two to four words costs about 17.5% of replies. 2–4 words yields around 46% open rates
versus 34% for ten words. Mobile truncates around 30–35 characters anyway.

**Lowercase.** All-lowercase has the highest open rates across 85M+ emails. It reads as
personal and internal. It also implies a human typed it quickly.

**Internal camouflage.** A subject that looks like it came from a colleague roughly
doubles open rates. "reply rates," "hiring ops," "Q2 forecast," "trial delays," "new
patients," "second page."

**Loss framing.** People respond more to a threatened loss than a potential gain.
"nick, you're wasting 2,300 per month" outperforms the gain-framed equivalent, as long
as the teaser doesn't give away the answer.

**Include a name somewhere.** Either the subject or the teaser should carry their name
or something equally specific. Not the whole subject — first name alone as a subject
reads as automation and costs about 12% of replies. But a name plus something else
works.

**Never no subject.** Empty subjects gain about 30% more opens but lose 12% of replies,
and they correlate strongly with spam and scams.

**Different subject on every follow-up.** Free A/B testing across the sequence.

## What works

```
nick
nick, are you hiring?
nick's blind spot
been with you from the start
2026 capital
your claude course + a formatting thought
you're wasting 2,300/mo
reply rates
new patients
second page
```

Why these work: they're short, they don't sell, and each one raises a question the
reader can only answer by opening. "nick's blind spot" — what blind spot? "2026
capital" — is someone trying to invest? "been with you from the start" — nobody
capitalizes it that way, so a person wrote it.

## What doesn't

```
Quick idea for your daily strategy content     - too specific, gives it away
Quick collab?                                  - "quick" signals low effort and a pitch
Can you double this week?                      - meaningless
Interested in AI-driven performance optimization? - spelling out "AI" instead of "AI" as insiders write it, plus obviously a pitch
Video Editor                                   - role-as-subject, seen a thousand times
Nick, you scaled to 72k a month with automation, here's the next level  - pure sell
(no subject)                                   - spam-correlated
```

The word "quick" is a tell on its own. If you'd genuinely put thought into reaching
someone, you wouldn't preface it with "quick."

---

## Anti-patterns and their cost

| Anti-pattern | Impact |
| --- | --- |
| Salesy words: increase, boost, ROI | −17.9% opens |
| Urgency words: ASAP, urgent | Below 36% opens |
| Excessive punctuation: !!! ?? | −36% opens |
| Numbers and percentages | −46% opens |
| Emojis | Hurts B2B credibility |
| Pitching the product | −57% replies |
| First name as the entire subject | −12% replies |
| Empty subject | +30% opens, −12% replies |
| Spam triggers: free, guarantee, act now | Deliverability risk |
| Fake Re: or Fwd: | Destroys trust on open |

Note the tension: loss framing with a number ("you're wasting 2,300/mo") uses a number,
which the data says costs opens. It works anyway because the loss frame overpowers it.
Treat the numbers rule as a default, not a law, and test it.

## Questions

The data conflicts. One source says questions perform well; another says they cut opens
by 56%. The resolution: **specific pain questions work, generic questions fail.**

Works: "need help with {{specific challenge}}?" / "nick, are you hiring?"
Fails: "quick question?" / "have 15 minutes?" / "can we chat?"

Default to statements.

---

## The sender / subject / teaser system

Most people optimize only the subject line. Before the email is opened, the reader
evaluates at least five things. All of them are levers.

**1. Sender name (~20 characters).** First and last name by default. It signals where
you're from, whether you're a person or a brand, and how formal you are. If the full
name reads as intimidating for the audience, adjust.

**2. Subject line (~30–50 characters).**

**3. Preview teaser (~50–100 characters).**

Subject and teaser share a combined budget of roughly 150 characters, and they trade
off against each other. A short subject leaves more room for the teaser. Write them
together as one unit, because they're read as one unit.

Any unused characters get filled with metadata junk from the mail client ("On Tuesday,
March 17th..."). So there is no reason not to use the full budget. Keep the email body
at least 150 characters so the preview doesn't render as awkward white space.

**4. Profile picture.**

**5. Sender email address.** A scraped-looking address undermines a well-written email.

Work top to bottom when a campaign underperforms: is this the best sender name? subject?
teaser? profile picture? email address? body? Even if no single element is exceptional,
a coherent package performs.

## C-suite

Executives get 300–400 emails a day and decide in seconds. When an email passes their
filter, they reply about 23% more often than non-C-suite (6.4% versus 5.2%).

What passes: ultra-concise, human, understated. A specific project name, a trigger
event, "a question," "thank you," "an update." Anything that reads as sales gets
rejected immediately.

---

## Teaser construction

The teaser is the first line of the body, so it's usually your personalization line.
Same rule applies: it must not resolve the curiosity the subject created.

```
Subject: your thumbs
Teaser:  Hi Nick, love your channel man. I've watched so many of your...
```

Subject raises the question. Teaser proves a human wrote it. Neither answers "who is
this and what do they want." That answer is inside the email, which is where you want
them.
